#+TITLE: cl-jobpond
#+STARTUP: content

=cl-jobpond= runs caller-supplied job bodies on a bounded pool of reusable
threads. Every job is supervised: it publishes exactly one terminal result, it
can be cancelled safely while it runs, it can carry a wall-clock deadline, and it
reports progress to listeners along the way.

#+BEGIN_SRC lisp
(let ((pool (cl-jobpond:make-job-pool :maximum-concurrency 4)))
  (unwind-protect
       (let ((job (cl-jobpond:job-pool-submit
                   pool
                   (lambda (job)
                     (* 2 (cl-jobpond:job-payload job)))
                   :name "double"
                   :payload 21)))
         (getf (cl-jobpond:job-await job) :result))
    (cl-jobpond:job-pool-close pool)))
;; => 42
#+END_SRC

The pool knows nothing about what a job does. It owns admission, concurrency,
lifecycle, cancellation, and retention; the body owns the work.

* Jobs

A job body is a function of one argument, the job itself. Its outcome is typed:

- returning a value publishes =:completed= with that value as the result,
- signalling =job-aborted= publishes =:aborted=,
- signalling any other error publishes =:failed= with a bounded condition report.

A body that escapes even those paths, for instance by signalling a
=serious-condition= that is not an error, is caught by the worker backstop and
published as =:failed=. A job that has been admitted always becomes terminal.

=job-snapshot= returns a coherent plist of state, result, cancellation reason,
condition report, timings, and progress. Reading the individual accessors is
cheaper but can mix values from either side of a transition, so prefer the
snapshot when several fields must agree.

* Admission

=job-pool-submit= admits one job. =job-pool-submit-batch= admits a list of entry
plists atomically: either every entry is queued, or none is and the pool is
untouched.

#+BEGIN_SRC lisp
(cl-jobpond:job-pool-submit-batch
 pool
 (list (list :function #'summarize :payload report-a :name "report-a")
       (list :function #'summarize :payload report-b :name "report-b")))
#+END_SRC

Three bounds are enforced. =:maximum-concurrency= limits jobs running at the same
time, =:maximum-batch-size= limits one atomic admission, and
=:maximum-live-jobs= limits queued and running jobs together. The last two refuse
work with =job-pool-capacity-exceeded= rather than letting a queue grow without
limit.

A pool starts one worker thread per unit of its concurrency bound at
construction, so a pool costs its threads from the moment it exists. Close it
with =job-pool-close= when it is no longer needed.

* Progress

A body calls =job-report-progress= to publish a progress value, append to a
bounded output tail, and advance a step count. =job-progress-snapshot= reads the
result from any thread.

#+BEGIN_SRC lisp
(cl-jobpond:job-report-progress job :detail '(:stage :parsing) :output line)
#+END_SRC

Every progress report is also a cancellation point: it signals =job-aborted= when
a controller asked for cancellation. A body that reports progress regularly
therefore stops cleanly without ever being interrupted.

* Cancellation

=job-cancel= is first-writer. The first accepted request records its reason;
later requests return =nil=. A queued job leaves the queue and publishes
=:aborted= immediately. A running job is interrupted on its worker thread.

An interrupt is prepared on the controller's thread and delivered later, by which
time the worker may have finished the target job and started a different one. Each
execution therefore carries a unique run token, and the interrupt only unwinds
when the worker still reports the same job, the same token, and is not in the
middle of publishing its terminal result. That guard is what stops a late
interrupt from killing an unrelated job that merely inherited the worker.

Interrupts are best effort. A body blocked where the host cannot deliver them
stops at its next =job-check-cancellation= or =job-report-progress= call instead.
=job-aborted= is a =serious-condition= rather than an =error=, so a body that
handles =error= broadly cannot accidentally swallow its own cancellation.

* Deadlines

A positive =:maximum-runtime-milliseconds= gives every job of the pool a
wall-clock cap, and an individual entry may override it. A single monitor thread
cancels expired jobs with the reason =:timeout=. Zero disables deadlines, and a
pool that can never have one runs no monitor thread at all.

* Terminal results

Terminal jobs stay available for inspection through =job-pool-find-job= and
=job-pool-list-jobs=, bounded by =:terminal-retention-limit=. Once the ring is
full the oldest terminal job is evicted, and looking it up afterwards signals
=job-not-found=. The evicted job object itself remains valid for anyone still
holding it.

Publication is single-writer. A body returning normally, a deadline, and pool
shutdown can all race for the same job, and exactly one of them installs the
terminal result. A job cancelled just as it finished publishes =:aborted= with no
result rather than reporting a success nobody wanted.

A host that wants to own what a terminal job *carries* supplies
=:terminal-result-function=. It is called with the job, the resolved state, the
result being published, and the report, and returns its own result, report, and
state:

#+BEGIN_SRC lisp
(lambda (job state result report)
  (values (list :status state :value result :error report)
          report
          state))
#+END_SRC

The hook runs inside the publication claim and outside the job's lifecycle lock,
so exactly one writer ever runs it and it may take locks or do input and output.
That is what makes it the right place for work whose side effects must not be
duplicated, such as writing a result artifact named after the job. It runs on
every publication path, including a job cancelled before it ever started, so a
host never has to reconstruct a record the pool published without it. A state it
returns is honoured only when that state is itself terminal, so a hook cannot
revive a job.

* Listeners

=job-pool-add-listener= registers a function of a channel keyword and an event
plist. The pool emits =:job-lifecycle= events with the statuses =:started=,
=:completed=, =:failed=, and =:aborted=, and =:job-progress= events for each
report. Every event carries the job it is about as =:job=, because a listener
wanting more than the status has to reach the job for it, and looking one up by
identifier would fail exactly when retention has already evicted it.

A listener that signals is ignored, so an observer can never turn a job into a
pool failure. Listeners run on the thread that produced the event, so they must
not block.

* Shutdown

=job-pool-close= cancels every admitted job, stops every thread, and returns
=t= only when the pool reached =:closed=. It is graceful in that every job
becomes terminal and every waiter is woken, but it is not a drain: a running job
is stopped rather than finished. Wait for the jobs that matter with =job-await=
first.

Closing is idempotent and safe from several threads at once; one caller becomes
the close owner and the others wait for its result. =job-pool-detach= then drops
the runtime state of a closed pool, and =job-pool-refresh= reopens it with fresh
workers.

* Job trees

A job may name its ancestors with =:owner-identifiers=, outermost first, and the
tree it belongs to with =:root-identifier=. A job is a descendant of every
identifier in its owner list, so =job-pool-descendant-jobs= finds a whole subtree
in one pass however deep it is, and =job-cancel= with =:cascade-p= cancels that
subtree and returns the descendants it took. The job itself is cancelled first, so a body that spawns children stops
adding to the subtree before the subtree is walked; a descendant admitted after
the walk is not reached, so a caller needing a guaranteed empty subtree cancels
and then re-checks.

* Host fields

A host with fields of its own subclasses =job= and gives the pool
=:job-class=; each admission entry then carries =:initargs= for that class. That
keeps host fields in real slots with real accessors rather than inside
=:payload=, which is what a host wants once it has more than a value or two to
carry.
